Wednesday evening found me heading west on the 85 along Carling Avenue, the October grey giving way to a surprisingly warm evening sun. My destination was a Filipino restaurant tucked into a strip mall at 2916 Carling Avenue, the kind of place you could easily drive past without giving it a second thought.

But The PH Kitchen is much more than a restaurant.

Since opening about six years ago, the owners have gradually bought up several spaces in the plaza, turning what was once an ordinary strip mall into something of a Filipino community hub. There’s a grocery store, a barbershop and, of course, the kitchen and dining area serving food from across the Philippines. Strip mall culture is alive and thriving.

I arrived around 5 p.m. and was soon joined by my friends Dina and Paula, whom I hadn’t seen in a while. We decided the best approach was to order a little bit of everything and see where the meal took us.

The restaurant had recently undergone renovations, and the difference was noticeable. It was bright, spacious and clean, with rows of red chairs and plenty of tables. Harvey, one of the co-owners, greeted me while I waited for the others and began telling me the story of how this little operation had grown.

The PH Kitchen started as a grocery store. As the years went by, customers began asking for more food and more opportunities to sit down and eat. Eventually, the owners bought a small pizza shop at the end of the plaza, which had only about 10 seats, and transformed it into the kitchen.

At one point, customers were eating their meals in their cars because there simply wasn’t enough room inside.

That was never really the plan. It was a response to what the community wanted.

The owners continued growing through the pandemic, buying the store without knowing what the coming years would bring. But food, Harvey told me, was a vital business, so they stayed open. The customer base kept following them, and what began as a simple grocery operation became something considerably larger.

Today, it’s essentially a one-stop shop. You can buy groceries, grab hot food, get a haircut and sit down for a meal, all without leaving the plaza.

There is something wonderfully familiar about the atmosphere in Filipino establishments. There’s a warmth and an easy joy to the room that makes you feel like you’ve arrived somewhere you’re supposed to be. I had a feeling we were going to eat well, but I also knew we were going to laugh a lot. I was right.

Harvey and his co-owner Jay have built the business around that sense of family and community. Most of their customers are Filipino, but increasingly people from other backgrounds are finding their way here through word of mouth and referrals.

Their ambitions extend well beyond Carling Avenue. They are planning another location in Orléans and already have plans for Montreal, with a broader goal of bringing Filipino food to communities across Canada.

For Harvey, that means more than simply putting dishes on a menu. He’s originally from the southern Philippines, while the staff come from different parts of the country. That diversity is deliberate, helping the restaurant represent the food of the islands rather than one particular region.

It’s a distinction I appreciated as we worked our way through the food.

We started with tortang talong, the Filipino eggplant omelette, along with fish with bagoong, the savoury fermented shrimp paste that brings a distinctive depth to the dish. There was also pork stir-fry with broccoli and giniling, a comforting minced pork dish that I particularly enjoyed, especially with the saltiness and little quail eggs mixed in.

Then came the pork satay kebab.

![Pork satay kebab: Tender pork skewers coated in a sweet, savoury barbecue sauce with a hint of peanut-like richness — simple, delicious and anything but chewy](/uploads/img_3388.jpeg "The PH Kitchen pork satay kebab")

This was one of my favourites. The pork was tender rather than chewy, with a sweet, savoury barbecue sauce coating each piece. There was a subtle peanut-like quality to the sauce that made it particularly satisfying. It’s a simple idea — meat on a stick — but when it’s done this well, you don’t need much more.

Dina was especially taken with the fish. It reminded her of properly prepared fish from when she was younger, and she loved the texture. The flavour was refreshing and paired beautifully with the accompanying dishes. We agreed that if it had arrived a little hotter, the skin might have had an even better crispness, but there was still something comforting about it.

And then Dina made an observation that I hadn’t really considered before: one of the marks of a good restaurant is often the rice.

She was right.

The rice here was excellent — perfectly sticky and exactly what you want alongside stir-fries, curries and saucier meats. It sounds like a small thing, but these are often the details that make a meal work. Good food isn’t always about the dramatic dish sitting in the centre of the table. Sometimes it’s about everything around it being just right.

Harvey had also been talking up the halo-halo, and by this point we were already very full. Halo-halo is a classic Filipino dessert made by layering sweetened fruits, beans, jellies and other ingredients with shaved ice and milk, often topped with ice cream or other treats.

Ours was a little dessert universe of its own.

Sweet, crunchy and wonderfully textured, it had layers of different ingredients that kept revealing something new with every spoonful. There were even corn flakes, which added a fantastic crunch against everything else. We were already deep into a food coma, but somehow the three of us managed to finish it.

That says something.

By the time we left, we had eaten roughly half a dozen dishes plus dessert, all for around $80. Looking over the menu, there are also combinations that come in at around $12, making this the kind of place where you can eat generously without turning dinner into an occasion that requires a budget meeting.

But the price is only part of the appeal.

What I really love about The PH Kitchen is that the restaurant has become the nucleus of something much bigger. This is a strip mall you might otherwise drive past without a second glance, yet inside there is a grocery store, a barbershop, a kitchen, families coming in for food and a staff drawn from different parts of the Philippines.

There is life here.

And that seems to be exactly what Harvey and Jay are trying to create. The food is the reason people walk through the door, but the culture and community are what make them keep coming back.

We’ll certainly be back. Next time, we’ll arrive earlier — by the time we visited, several dishes had already sold out — and I’m determined to try the pancit and some of the Filipino dishes I’ve never encountered before.

There’s still a lot of the Philippines to eat.

And thankfully, The PH Kitchen is bringing more of it to Ottawa.
